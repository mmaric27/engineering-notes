# How I approach team wellbeing and development

When I led two delivery teams, I used one-to-ones to understand how people felt about their work, what support they needed and where they wanted to develop. I also reviewed delivery data. I kept the two conversations distinct: finishing work on time tells me something about delivery, but very little about what it took out of the person doing it.

## A useful one-to-one

“Are you happy with your work?” is a fair question, but “yes, mostly” gives us little to act on. I prefer something concrete: **“Over the past month, what would you have liked more time for? What kept taking its place?”**

Consider a hypothetical answer: “I like helping people debug things. What bothers me is that I’m always the first person they ask. I haven’t had a stretch of time to design anything in months.”

It would be easy to hear *too many interruptions* and suggest a support rotation. But the engineer hasn’t said they want to stop helping. They’ve said that being useful in one way is crowding out work they want to do. Before proposing a fix, I’d ask which part they want to keep and which part they want to change.

Suppose we agree they’ll own the design of an upcoming integration, and I’ll arrange for someone else to take first-line questions while they work on it. We now both have a responsibility. The engineer has a real design decision to make. My job is to make that time available, not advise them to “protect their focus” while continuing to send people to them.

At the next one-to-one, I’d start with our agreement: **“Did you get to own the design, or did we just add it on top of everything else?”** That answer is more useful than a satisfaction score. It also tells the engineer whether raising a concern with me is worth doing again.

## Three perspectives worth keeping separate

| Perspective | What it helps me understand | What it cannot tell me |
|---|---|---|
| The team member | Whether the work is satisfying, whether support is available and whether there is room to grow. | How the rest of the team feels. |
| Colleagues, customers and stakeholders | Where collaboration works and where people encounter friction. | What it costs the team to keep that collaboration working. |
| Delivery evidence | Patterns in interruptions, unfinished work, defects and time to delivery. | Whether an individual is motivated, developing or performing well. |

The interesting case is when all three accounts are true. Colleagues may praise an engineer for always being available. Delivery may be on track. The engineer may still want their role to change. I wouldn’t use the first two observations to argue against the third.

## What stays private

The team may need to hear that support requests depend too heavily on one person. It does not need to hear what that person told me about their satisfaction or career plans. I can take responsibility for changing how requests are handled without identifying who raised the concern. For follow-up, the useful record is the change we agreed to, who will do what and when we’ll revisit it. It doesn’t need to be a transcript.

For me, the test of this approach is simple: when someone tells me what they need to do good work, **does anything change in their working day?**
